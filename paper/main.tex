\title{When First-Token Readouts Misread Feature Steering in a Medical Vision--Language Model}

\author{Kevin Jin\\Duke University\\\texttt{kevin.jin@duke.edu}}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Interventions on vision--language models are often scored on yes/no questions by the model's first
output token. A common score is the probability of ``yes'' normalized over ``yes'' and ``no'', the
probability form of the logit difference that interpretability work recommends. We show that this
score can miss most of an intervention's effect on the answer the model writes. In MedGemma 1.5
(4B), we train a transcoder, a sparse dictionary of the model's internal features with
\val{clt.used}B trained parameters, and suppress its $80$ most active features, by reversing their sign, on \val{n.fp} chest
radiographs that the model calls positive for cardiomegaly although their labels are negative. The
normalized score barely moves (\val{fp.base.norm} to \val{fp.scale1.norm}), but the share of sampled
answers that say yes falls from \val{fp.base.s} to \val{fp.scale1.s}. Across three versions of the
edit, the greedy answer switches to ``no'' \val{pf.clamp.gno} times, and the score still
says yes on \val{pf.clamp.gnoyes} of them. Three ways of occluding the image region the call depends on
switch \val{pf.occ.gno} answers, and the score misses only \val{pf.occ.gnoyes}. The generated text shows why: after
suppression, the model more often opens with ``Based on the image, \ldots'' instead of ``Yes'', and
that reasoning more often ends in ``no''. The first-token score sees neither change. The gap
replicates on radiographs selected by their written answer rather than the score, and on correct
effusion and pneumothorax calls
(correct cardiomegaly calls barely respond to the edit). We hypothesize that it comes from how
circuit-tracing tools apply edits, which constrain the first token but not the rest of the answer.
An edit that looks inert by this score can still change what the model tells a clinician, so such
interventions should be scored on sampled answers. We release the transcoder and code.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A simple way to ask a vision--language model (VLM) about an image is a closed question: ``Does this
chest X-ray show cardiomegaly? Answer yes or no.'' The answer is usually scored from the first output
token. The probability of ``yes'' is used to score image--text agreement \citep{lin2024vqascore}, and
interpretability studies score an intervention by how much it changes that probability. Examples
include causal tracing \citep{meng2022rome,palit2023blip,basu2024storage}, attention knockout in
multimodal question answering \citep{zhang2025crossmodal}, and, in medical VLMs, activation steering
\citep{farina2026steering} and sparse-autoencoder feature patching \citep{sadanandan2026b}.

The probability of ``yes'' has a known weakness: a model can put next-token probability on tokens
that are not answers \citep{wiegreffe2023mass}. An instruction-tuned model may open with ``Sure'' or
a refusal, and its first-token probabilities can disagree with the answer it writes
\citep{wang2024answerc,lyu2024beyond}. Interpretability work therefore recommends the logit difference
between the two answers \citep{zhang2024patching,heimersheim2024patching,tan2024steering}, or its
probability form, normalized over the answers \citep{rimsky2024caa}. Both assume that the model
decides at the answer position. A model that opens with ``Based on the image, \ldots'' decides only
after reasoning, and that answer can differ from a direct one \citep{wang2024cotdecoding}. A
first-token readout cannot see how such reasoning ends. In a clinical tool, the user reads the
written answer, so a readout that misses changes to it misreports the behavior that matters.

We test this in MedGemma 1.5. We steer it by suppressing features of a transcoder, a learned
dictionary that rewrites the model's internal activations as a sparse set of more interpretable
features, and compare three first-token readouts with the answers the model writes. On one
radiograph after suppression, the normalized probability of ``yes'' is \val{ex.norm}, yet the model
writes ``\textit{Based on the image, the chest X-ray shows a normal heart size}'' and answers no. As a
reference, we occlude the image region that the call depends on, an edit the readouts should
register. Figure~\ref{fig:split} shows both.

We apply the edit as circuit-tracing tools do \citep{ameisen2025circuit}. While the model reads the
prompt, each feed-forward (MLP) block keeps the output it computed on the unedited input, plus the edit's
decoded change, instead of recomputing from its changed input; we call this the \emph{clamp}. The first answer token is predicted at the last prompt position, where the clamp
holds the MLP blocks fixed. Every later token is predicted at a new position, whose MLP blocks
respond to the edit. We hypothesize that this mismatch lets the first token miss what the rest of
the answer says, and test it by also editing the \emph{live model}, in which every MLP block recomputes from its
input. Each test's decision rule was
committed before its run; post hoc comparisons are marked~\dag.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{The readout misses the edit's effect on the answers.} Suppressing the $80$ transcoder
features most active on the image turns \val{pf.clamp.gno} greedy answers to ``no'', and the
normalized readout still says yes on \val{pf.clamp.gnoyes}\dag. Occluding the image region the call
depends on turns \val{pf.occ.gno}, and the readout misses \val{pf.occ.gnoyes}\dag. Pre-registered tests confirm the gap, including on radiographs that no
first-token readout selected (\S\ref{sec:clamp}).
\item \textbf{Two routes, and a candidate cause.} Suppression shifts answers from an immediate
``Yes'' to reasoning first, and makes the reasoning end in ``no'' more often. The normalized readout
registers neither, and the raw probability of ``yes'' matches the answers only by coincidence
(\S\ref{sec:routes}). We hypothesize that the clamp is why. Without it, at the only strength that met
the pre-registered criteria, the first token moves with the answers, but the test is not conclusive (\S\ref{sec:live}).
\item \textbf{Scope.} The gap recurs on correct effusion and pneumothorax calls. Correct cardiomegaly
calls barely respond to the edit, so the pre-registered test of generalisation is inconclusive. The
same selection rule picks nearly the same features for every finding: the edit is a generic perturbation of
image features, not removal of cardiomegaly evidence (\S\ref{sec:scope}).
\end{enumerate}
These results come from one model, one transcoder, one dataset and tens of radiographs per group.
They show that a recommended readout can miss an intervention's effect in a realistic medical
setting, and by what route, but not how often this affects published results. We release the
transcoder, the code, and a result file behind every
number.\footnote{Code and results: \url{https://github.com/kevinjin918/first-token-readouts}.
Transcoder: \url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22105454}.}

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Model, data and prompt.} We steer MedGemma 1.5-4b-it \citep{medgemma2026,medgemma2025}
in bfloat16 on a $4{,}999$-image subset of NIH ChestX-ray14 \citep{wang2017chestxray} ($1{,}335$
patients). Each radiograph is presented in a single turn with the question ``Does this chest X-ray
show \textit{finding}? Answer yes or no.'' ChestX-ray14 labels are mined from radiology reports and
are noisy. We use them only to select radiographs; every steering comparison compares the model with
itself.

\paragraph{First-token readouts.} Let $\ell$ be the logits at the position where the answer would
begin and $p = \operatorname{softmax}(\ell)$. For each answer we use the token with the largest
logit among the first tokens of its surface forms (\say{yes}, \say{\textvisiblespace yes},
\say{Yes}, \say{\textvisiblespace Yes}, \say{YES}, and likewise for ``no''), and define
\[
\Praw = p_{\text{yes}}, \qquad
m = p_{\text{yes}} + p_{\text{no}}, \qquad
\Pnorm = \frac{p_{\text{yes}}}{p_{\text{yes}} + p_{\text{no}}} = \sigma(\Delta), \quad
\Delta = \ell_{\text{yes}} - \ell_{\text{no}}.
\]
$\Praw$ is the probability that the next token is ``yes'', and the \emph{answer mass} $m$
approximates the probability that it is an answer at all, so $\Praw = \Pnorm\cdot m$. $\Pnorm$ is a
sigmoid of the yes-minus-no logit margin $\Delta$: the probability form of the logit difference
recommended for activation patching \citep{zhang2024patching,heimersheim2024patching}. On our
radiographs $\Pnorm$ starts near $1$ (median $\Delta$ of \val{fp.base.gapmedian} nats, odds of about
\val{fp.base.gapodds} to $1$), so we also report $\Delta$ and how many films each readout puts below
$0.5$.

\paragraph{Written answers.} For each radiograph and intervention, we decode greedily and draw
\val{e4.nsamples} samples at temperature $1$ (no top-$k$ or nucleus truncation), up to
\val{e4.maxtok} new tokens. A fixed rule removes markdown symbols, lowercases, and takes the first
whole word ``yes'' or ``no''; a text with neither gives neither answer. The \emph{sampled yes rate}
$s$ is the fraction of samples that say yes. A sample \emph{answers directly} if its first word is
``yes'' or ``no'', and \emph{reasons first} otherwise. A blind hand reading agreed with the rule on
\val{hand.e4.agree} of \val{hand.e4.n} random samples. The rule errs toward ``neither'', and counting
such answers as yes or as no changes only one verdict (Appendix~\ref{app:parse}).

\paragraph{The edit.} A cross-layer transcoder (CLT) reads the input to each MLP block and
reconstructs the outputs of that block and all later ones from a sparse set of features
\citep{lindsey2024crosscoders,ameisen2025circuit}. Ours covers all $34$ layers with $2{,}048$
features per layer and was trained on activations from radiographs and, separately, report texts
(Appendix~\ref{app:instrument}). On each radiograph we pick the $80$ features with the highest mean
activation over the image tokens and edit them there. \texttt{scale}~$\lambda$ multiplies a
feature's activation by $\lambda$ ($\lambda=-1$ reverses its sign, $\lambda=-2$ reverses and doubles
it), and \texttt{ablate} sets it to zero. To apply an edit, we run the unedited prompt and record, at
every position and layer, the transcoder's features and its reconstruction error (the error term of
\citealp{ameisen2025circuit}). We then rerun the prompt with each MLP output replaced by the
reconstruction from the recorded features, edited at image positions, plus the recorded error. With
no edit this reproduces the model exactly. With an edit, each MLP output equals its unedited value
plus the decoded change; it is not recomputed from its changed input, while attention is recomputed
throughout. This is the clamp. The readouts are taken at the last prompt position, a text position
whose MLP outputs keep their unedited values, so the edit reaches them only through attention.
Constrained patching \citep{ameisen2025circuit} additionally freezes attention over a range of
layers, but likewise leaves the MLP blocks at the readout position unable to respond to the edit,
the property our hypothesis concerns. The circuit-tracer library \citep{hanna2025circuittracer} likewise applies its constraints
only while predicting the first new token.\footnote{The documentation of
\texttt{feature\_intervention\_generate} states that the constraints ``will only apply to the very
first token generated''. Later tokens attend to the cached states of the constrained pass.} The
first generated token has the readouts' distribution (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}), so the readouts and
the text can part ways only after the first token.

\paragraph{Radiographs.} The main comparison uses \val{n.fp} radiographs (\emph{films}) that the
model calls positive for cardiomegaly although ChestX-ray14 labels them negative: false positives
with respect to the label, which no radiologist checked. All are anteroposterior (AP), a projection
that magnifies the heart relative to the standard posteroanterior view, so the heart can look
enlarged when it is not. Chest-radiograph classifiers are known to pick up how a film was acquired,
such as a portable unit \citep{zech2018} and, probably, the projection \citep{degrave2021}. The call
then plausibly rests on the heart's apparent size, so we can check where the model's
evidence lies; nothing below requires the call to be right or wrong. The films come from
\val{n.fppatients} patients, in two sets of \val{n.fpbanked} with no patient in both. The first set,
from our pilot study (the version of this work first submitted, which scored first-token readouts
only), was selected by $\Praw \geq 0.5$. The
second was selected by the greedy answer being yes, so that no readout under test chose it
(Appendix~\ref{app:sets}). For correct calls, we drew \val{n.tpcardiomegaly} radiographs each for
cardiomegaly, pleural effusion (fluid around the lung) and pneumothorax (air around the lung),
labelled positive and answered yes, one film per patient and no patient in two sets.

\paragraph{Pre-registration.} Each test we call pre-registered had its decision rule committed in its
script before the run. We state outcomes in plain words with the rule's label in small capitals;
Appendix~\ref{app:rules} gives each rule, outcome and amendment.

\section{A reference effect: occluding the evidence}
\label{sec:evidence}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{fig_location.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Where the evidence for each call sits.} \textbf{(a)} For each of the first \val{loc.n}
false-positive radiographs, the $2\times2$-token block whose black occlusion most lowers $\Praw$,
counted on the $8\times8$ block grid and drawn over the mean of the \val{loc.n} images. All
\val{loc.central} fall over the cardiac silhouette and the mediastinum. \textbf{(b)} One of the
\val{loc.n}: blacking out the outlined block, at the left heart border, takes $\Pnorm$ from
\val{loc.ex.base} to \val{loc.ex.black}. \textbf{(c)} The most damaging block on a separate set of
\val{attn.tpeff.n} effusion- and \val{attn.tpptx.n} pneumothorax-positive radiographs from the pilot
study, which may include several films per patient, for films where one block moves $\Pnorm$ by
more than $0.15$. Effusion blocks lie at the lung bases; pneumothorax blocks lie in the upper zones
or, on three films from one patient, over a chest tube.}
\label{fig:location}
\end{figure}

To know what a readout should register, we need an edit that changes the call by removing its
evidence. MedGemma encodes each image as a $16\times16$ grid of tokens. We occlude one $2\times2$
block of that grid (one sixty-fourth of the image) at a time and record the change in the
first-token readout. The block with the largest effect is the film's \emph{evidence
block}.\footnote{For the first \val{loc.n} films, the block that most lowers $\Praw$, as chosen in
the pilot study; for the films drawn for this paper, the block that most lowers $\Delta$.}

What do these blocks cover? On the false positives, anatomy whose apparent size depends on how the
film was taken. On \val{loc.central} of the first \val{loc.n} films the block lies in the central
$4\times4$ blocks, and on \val{loc.leftheart} in the two columns just right of the image midline, the
patient's left, where most of the cardiac silhouette lies (Figure~\ref{fig:location}a,b). So the call
rests on the apparent size of the heart, which the AP projection inflates, rather than on an
abnormality recorded in the label. On other findings the blocks mostly cover the pathology: effusion blocks lie at
the lung bases and pneumothorax blocks in the upper zones (Figure~\ref{fig:location}c). On $3$
of the \val{attn.tpptx.nrem} pneumothorax films, all from one patient, the block lies on the curled tip
of a chest tube, the treatment-device shortcut documented by
\citet{oakdenrayner2020}.

Occluding the evidence block changes the answers, and the first-token readouts follow
(Table~\ref{tab:main}, lower rows). Blacking it out turns the greedy answer to ``no'' on
\val{fp.black.greedyno} of \val{n.fp} films and lowers the sampled yes rate from \val{fp.base.s} to
\val{fp.black.s}; $\Pnorm$ says no on \val{fp.black.flipsnorm} films. Three controls tie the effect to
the evidence rather than to the occlusion. An identical black square at a random border block leaves
the answers in place, so the effect is not the size of the perturbation. Mean fill, which leaves no
sharp edge, still changes the answers, so it is not an edge artifact. And on the \val{occ.eff.n}
films of the first set also called positive for effusion, occluding the cardiac block leaves that
call at $\Pnorm \geq \val{occ.eff.minnorm}$, so it is not general damage to the model. By the
rule we pre-registered for suppression (Table~\ref{tab:v1}), applied here as a reference\dag, no
first-token readout falls short of the answers when the evidence is removed.

\paragraph{What the first token says.} Read through $\Pnorm$, suppression leaves the model's call
nearly unchanged.\footnote{Our pilot study scored first-token readouts only and drew this
conclusion. The written answers below show that the call does change.} We report
\texttt{scale}~$-1$; \texttt{ablate} and \texttt{scale}~$-2$ agree throughout (Tables~\ref{tab:main}
and~\ref{tab:v1}). Suppression lowers $\Praw$ from \val{fp.base.raw2} to \val{fp.scale1.raw2}, but
almost none of the lost probability goes to ``no'' (mean $p_{\text{no}}$ at most
\val{fp.clamp.pnomax} under every variant). It goes to ``Based'' (Figure~\ref{fig:split}). $\Pnorm$
therefore moves only from \val{fp.base.norm} to \val{fp.scale1.norm}, and says no on
\val{fp.scale1.flipsnorm} of \val{n.fp} films.

\paragraph{What the model writes.} On one film under \texttt{scale}~$-1$, $\Pnorm$ is \val{ex.norm}
and the model writes:
\begin{quote}\small
\val{ex.clamp}
\end{quote}
Unedited, the same film gets ``\val{ex.base}'' Summed over the three variants,
suppression turns \val{pf.clamp.gno} greedy answers to ``no'' (on \val{pf.clamp.gnofilms} distinct
films), and $\Pnorm$ still says yes on \val{pf.clamp.gnoyes} of them\dag, each time at
$\Pnorm \geq \val{pf.clamp.missmin}$. The three occlusions of the evidence block (Table~\ref{tab:main})
turn \val{pf.occ.gno}, and $\Pnorm$ misses \val{pf.occ.gnoyes}\dag, a comparison that favours the readout, since a
first-token readout chose the evidence blocks.

The sampled answers show the same gap, and the pre-registered tests confirm it. Under
\texttt{scale}~$-1$, the sampled yes rate falls from \val{fp.base.s} to \val{fp.scale1.s}. Under
each variant, $\Pnorm$ falls short of the drop in $s$ by \val{fp.clamp.minshortfall} to
\val{fp.clamp.maxshortfall}, a miss by the rule under all three (\textsc{misreads},
Table~\ref{tab:v1}). These verdicts also hold with non-answers set aside. An earlier
pre-registered check on the first \val{e2.n} films ($10$ samples each) had a simpler rule, met under all three variants: suppression turns the greedy
answer to ``no'' on at least $3$ films where $\Pnorm$ still says yes. On the \val{n.fpfresh} films
selected by greedy answer alone, which no first-token readout chose, the rule is met again
(\val{v.V2}).

\subsection{Two routes to the answer}
\label{sec:routes}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\footnotesize
\input{tables/routes}
\caption{\textbf{Sampled answers by their first word,} pooled over \val{n.fp} films and
\val{e4.nsamples} samples each. Answers that open with ``Yes'' or ``No'' give that answer by
construction. Suppression moves answers to the reasoning route and changes where that route ends.}
\label{tab:routes}
\end{table}

Why does the text move when $\Pnorm$ barely does? Sampled answers fall into two groups by their first
word (Table~\ref{tab:routes}). Some open with ``Yes'' or ``No'' and answer at once; almost all of
these say yes. The rest reason first, almost always opening with ``Based on the image''. Let $r$ be
the share of samples that reason first and $y$ the share of those that conclude yes. A sample opens
with ``Yes'' with probability about $\Praw$, so
\[
s \;\approx\; \Praw + r\,y .
\]
Suppression changes both how the model answers and what it concludes. It moves first-token
probability from ``Yes'' to ``Based'' (the probability of ``Based'' rises from \val{fp.base.based} to
\val{fp.scale1.based}), which
lowers $\Praw$ and raises $r$ from \val{route.base.reasoned} to \val{route.scale1.reasoned}. It also
lowers $y$ from \val{route.base.ryes} to \val{route.scale1.ryes}. $\Pnorm$ compares ``yes'' with
``no'' at the first token, where ``no'' is almost never chosen, so it registers neither change.
Depending on the variant, it falls by less than $0.01$ on \val{ceil.tinymin} to \val{ceil.tinymax}
of the \val{n.fp} films, while $s$ falls on those films by \val{ceil.sdroptinymin} to
\val{ceil.sdroptinymax} on average. The margin $\Delta$ does register the suppression: its median
change orders all seven interventions as the drop in $s$ does (Table~\ref{tab:main})\dag. But from a
median of \val{fp.base.gapmedian} nats it rarely crosses zero, because the lost ``yes'' probability
goes to ``Based'', which the margin leaves out, not to ``no''. The miss is therefore not just the
sigmoid saturating.

$\Praw$ falls by about as much as $s$ (\val{fp.scale1.rawdrop} against \val{fp.scale1.sdrop}), but by
coincidence. $\Praw$ is the first term alone, so $s - \Praw \approx r\,y$, and suppression changes
$r\,y$ little (from \val{route.base.ryesall} to \val{route.scale1.ryesall}, \val{route.ablate.ryesall}
and \val{route.scale2.ryesall} under the three variants) because it raises $r$ while lowering $y$. By the pre-registered rule, $\Praw$
tracks the answers under one variant and is unresolved under the other two (Table~\ref{tab:v1}); with non-answers
set aside, it overstates the effect under \texttt{scale}~$-1$ and $-2$ (by \val{fp.scale1.rawvsc} and
\val{fp.scale2.rawvsc}).

\subsection{Is the clamp the cause?}
\label{sec:live}

If the clamp is why the first token misses how the reasoning ends, editing the live model, in which
every MLP block responds, should move the first token with the answers. Strength alone does not close
the gap under the clamp: under \texttt{scale}~$-2$, $s$ falls by \val{fp.scale2.sdrop}, but $\Pnorm$
only to \val{fp.scale2.norm}. We ran two pre-registered live tests on the same films, feature sets
and seeds, adding each edit's change in the transcoder's output to the model's own MLP outputs
(Appendix~\ref{app:live}). At the strengths of \S\ref{sec:clamp}, the live edit is far stronger than
the clamp: \val{e8.none.min} to \val{e8.none.max} of samples give neither answer. $\Pnorm$ falls by
less than $s$ under two variants, but by more than $s$ under all three once non-answers are excluded
(\val{e8.verdict}). With most samples not answering, this test cannot decide. The second test weakened
the edit, scaling each feature by $\lambda$ from $0.9$ down to $0.15$ ($\lambda=0.6$ changes each
activation a fifth as much as \texttt{scale}~$-1$). A strength counted only if $s$ still fell
(interval above $0.05$) and at most \val{e9.tau} of samples failed to answer (gave neither answer or said
they could not see an image), the most under any intervention of \S\ref{sec:clamp}. The response is a cliff: $s$ falls by \val{e9.weak.sdrop} at
$\lambda = \val{e9.weak.c}$ and by \val{e9.sdrop} at $\lambda = 0.6$, the only strength that
qualified. There the first token moves with the answers. It puts \val{e9.pno} on ``no'', against at
most \val{fp.clamp.pnomax} under the clamp, and $\Pnorm$ and $s$ fall by about the same amount
(difference \val{e9.normvs}).

This is what the clamp hypothesis predicts, but it does not establish the clamp as the cause, for
three reasons. (i)~Dose: the qualifying strength moves the answers \val{e9.ratio} times as much as
the clamp, and no live strength as mild as the clamp still moved them, so the two are never compared
at a matched effect. (ii)~Precision: the interval is wider than the $\pm0.05$ the rule needs to call
agreement (\val{e9.verdict}). (iii)~Confound: the live edit frees the MLP blocks at image positions as
well as at the answer position. Freeing only the answer position's blocks would isolate the
hypothesis, and is the natural next test.

\section{Scope}
\label{sec:scope}

\paragraph{Correct calls and other findings.} We repeated \texttt{scale}~$-1$ and the evidence
occlusion on \val{n.tpcardiomegaly} correctly called positive films for each of three findings
(Table~\ref{tab:tp}). Suppression lowers $s$ by \val{tpeffusion.scale1.sdrop} for effusion and by
\val{tppneumothorax.scale1.sdrop} for pneumothorax, and by the pre-registered rule $\Pnorm$ misses the
effect on both (\textsc{misreads}), as on the false positives. For cardiomegaly, $s$ falls by only
\val{tpcardiomegaly.scale1.sdrop}, too little for the rule to separate $\Pnorm$ from the answers, so
the verdict on generalisation is \val{v.V3s}. With non-answers set aside, $\Pnorm$ tracks the answers
on cardiomegaly, and the verdict becomes \val{v.V3c}. We read this\dag\ as an edit too weak on these
calls for any readout to miss: blacking out the evidence block moves them ($s$ falls by
\val{tpcardiomegaly.black.sdrop}), but this suppression barely does.

\paragraph{What the edit removes.} The suppression is not specific to cardiomegaly; it acts as a
generic perturbation of image features. If the $80$ most active features were the model's
cardiomegaly evidence, the same rule would pick different features for other findings. We built
top-activation sets for cardiomegaly, effusion and pneumothorax from \val{e3.ndonor} \emph{donor}
radiographs each, used only to choose features, one per patient, with no patient shared across sets
or with the test films. The sets overlap almost completely: their mean pairwise Jaccard index is
\val{e3.jac.activation.80}, against \val{e3.jac.contrast.80} for sets selected by contrast between
positive and negative donors, and the gap holds for sets of $20$ and $320$ features
(Appendix~\ref{app:donors}). Top activation picks what is active on any
chest radiograph. Contrast sets built from larger groups (\val{e5.ndonor} positive against
\val{e5.ndonor} negative donors) are finding-specific (Jaccard \val{e5.jaccon} between the
cardiomegaly and effusion sets), but suppressing them barely changes the answers. On correct calls
of its own finding, neither set beats $80$ random active features by the pre-registered margin, nor
does the cardiomegaly set on the false positives (\val{e5.S1}, \val{e5.S2}; Table~\ref{tab:e5}). Their
features are all active on the false positives, but we did not record how strongly they fire, so
weak firing could explain the small effect. The readout failure of \S\ref{sec:clamp} does
not depend on the edit being specific.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{First-token readouts of interventions.} \citet{farina2026steering} steer the live MedGemma
and score it from the next-token ``Yes'' and ``No'' logits without parsing the text, noting that
steering changes their combined mass. \citet{sadanandan2026b} score sparse-autoencoder patching of
MedGemma by the yes-minus-no margin.

\paragraph{The choice of metric.} \citet{zhang2024patching} show that probability and logit
difference can lead to different conclusions in activation patching, and recommend the logit
difference. \citet{heimersheim2024patching} note that it is insensitive to components that move all
compared logits together, the blind spot we measure. Steering work on multiple-choice questions uses
the probability normalized over the options (in the released code of \citealp{rimsky2024caa}) or the
logit difference \citep{tan2024steering}, and \citet{tan2024steering} argue that the model decides at
the answer token; ours is a case where it does not. Steering vectors built from multiple-choice
questions can fail on open-ended answers \citep{pres2024reliable}, model edits look far stronger
under teacher forcing than in free generation \citep{yang2025mirage}, a first-token proxy for refusal
is less accurate than full completions \citep{arditi2024refusal}, and a circuit-tracing intervention
can reverse when attention is allowed to respond \citep{lindsey2025biology}. Concurrently,
\citet{wang2026knowing} find, in general-domain VLMs prompted to reason first, that a direction patch
that barely changes the first-position choice still lowers the accuracy of the generated answers by
$13$ to $16$ points. We add a medical case under a direct yes/no prompt, where readout and answer
disagree film by film; we show the routes by which they part, and identify a candidate condition: a
first token computed under constraints that do not apply to the rest of the answer.

\paragraph{First tokens and generated text.} Instruction-tuned models shown the options place nearly
all first-token probability on them \citep{wiegreffe2023mass}; MedGemma, told to answer yes or no,
often does not. Under image swaps in MedGemma-27B, first-token and generated answers agree on $91$ to
$99\%$ of cases \citep{cajas2026modalens}, while generated answers are the more robust of the two to
question perturbations \citep{wang2024lookattext}. Alternative first tokens open reasoning paths that
change the answer \citep{wang2024cotdecoding}, and steering can induce such reasoning
\citep{zhang2024steeringcot}, including through sparse-autoencoder features \citep{he2026triggering}.
In medical VLMs, \citet{nooralahzadeh2026universal} steer with sparse-autoencoder features and
evaluate generated reports.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Recommendation.} When an intervention is evaluated on a closed question, score sampled
answers, and report the first-token readouts and the share of answers that reason first alongside
them. Scoring the answers would have caught both failures we saw: under the clamp, the first token
missed how the reasoning ended, and under strong live edits, the readout reported a decision while
the model mostly stopped answering. The answer mass $m$ alone is not enough. A fall in $m$ shows that
the first token no longer carries most answers, but not which way they moved: it fell further under
black occlusion (\val{fp.black.mass}), where $\Pnorm$ followed the answers, than under suppression
(\val{fp.scale1.mass}). A greedy answer alone can hide a shift in the distribution
\citep{pres2024reliable}; here, \val{e4.nsamples} samples per film sufficed. Finally, our clamp, like
circuit-tracer's generation function, constrains only the pass that predicts the first token.
Whether or not that caused the gap here (\S\ref{sec:live}), the first token and the rest of the
answer should come from one configuration, or the difference should be stated.

\paragraph{When first-token readouts suffice.} When the model answers at the first token ($m$ near
$1$), the logit difference describes the decision and remains the right tool for the patching
studies it was recommended for. In our setting, too, the margin ranked the seven interventions as the answers
did\dag, though it could not tell what the model answered. We do not judge whether the model's cardiomegaly calls
are clinically right, or treat its written reasoning as an explanation of its computation
\citep{lanham2023faithfulness,cox2026precommit}.

\paragraph{Limitations.} We steer one model, with one transcoder, on one dataset. The steering
comparisons use \val{n.fp} false-positive and \val{n.tp} true-positive radiographs, and the
top-activation sets of \S\ref{sec:scope} use \val{e3.ndonor} donors each. No radiologist reviewed the
radiographs, labels, occlusion maps or generated text, and the anatomical descriptions are our own.
The parse rule reads the first ``yes'' or ``no'' within \val{e4.maxtok} tokens, and a model could
revise its answer later; the author did the hand reading, blind but not independent. Per-film effects
depend on near-ties at the top-$80$ cutoff (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}), so we report means and pooled
counts. Sampling at temperature $1$ without truncation is not how a deployed model decodes, but the
greedy answers tell the same story.

\section{Pre-registered decision rules}
\label{app:rules}

Each rule was committed in its experiment's script before the run, and the commit hashes are in the
result files. Verdicts are stated in the rule's own terms.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Generation check} (first check, $20$ films, $10$ samples). Per suppression variant:
\textsc{disagrees} if the greedy answer is ``no'' on at least $3$ films where $\Pnorm$ calls yes;
otherwise \textsc{hedge-dominated} if it gives neither answer on at least $5$; otherwise
\textsc{validated} if it matches the $\Pnorm$ call on at least $18$; otherwise \textsc{mixed}.
Result: \textsc{disagrees} under all three variants (\val{e2.scale1.greedyno}, \val{e2.ablate.greedyno}
and \val{e2.scale2.greedyno} films under \texttt{scale}~$-1$, \texttt{ablate} and \texttt{scale}~$-2$).
The script also required the unedited model and \texttt{scale}~$-1$ to reproduce the pilot study's
$\Praw$ within $0.02$ on every film. It was amended twice, without changing the films, arms, seed or
verdict rules. When \texttt{scale}~$-1$ failed on two of the first five films (a feature swap at the
top-$80$ boundary, Appendix~\ref{app:repro}), the requirement was kept for the unedited model only,
before any generated text was read. When the unedited model's $\Praw$ then drifted by
\val{e2.base.banked} on the seventh film, the requirement was dropped and the run restarted. This
second amendment was not blind: the greedy answers for the first six films had been seen.
\item \textbf{Replication with sampled answers} (\val{n.fp} false-positive films, \val{e4.nsamples}
samples). Per variant, the per-film difference between a readout's change and the change in $s$ is
bootstrapped ($10{,}000$ resamples). \textsc{misreads} if the $95\%$ interval excludes $0$ and the
mean exceeds $0.05$ in size; \textsc{tracks} if the interval lies within $\pm0.05$; otherwise
\textsc{unresolved}. $\Pnorm$ is \textsc{supported} as missing the effect if all three variants
misread, \textsc{refuted} if all three track, and \textsc{partial} otherwise; likewise $\Praw$.
Fresh films: the first check's rule, \textsc{replicates} if at least $2$ of $3$ variants disagree.
True positives, under \texttt{scale}~$-1$: a finding shows an effect if the interval of its drop in
$s$ excludes $0$; \textsc{generalises} if at least two findings show an effect and $\Pnorm$ misreads
on all of them, \textsc{does not generalise} if $\Pnorm$ tracks on any of them, otherwise
\textsc{inconclusive}. Every verdict is repeated with $s$ over answered samples only. Results:
$\Pnorm$ \val{v.V1norms} and $\Praw$ \val{v.V1raws} as missing the effect (answered only:
\val{v.V1normc}, \val{v.V1rawc}); fresh films \val{v.V2}; true positives \val{v.V3s} (answered only:
\val{v.V3c}).
\item \textbf{Contrastive selection} (\val{n.fp} false positives, and \val{n.tpcardiomegaly}
correct calls each for cardiomegaly and effusion; $80$ features per set, \texttt{scale}~$-1$,
$10{,}000$-resample film bootstrap). \textsc{selection rescues specificity} if, on correct
cardiomegaly calls, the cardiomegaly contrast set lowers $s$ by more than the effusion set (mean
above $0.10$, interval above $0$) and the converse holds on correct effusion calls;
\textsc{contrastive sets inert} if neither contrast set beats random active features by $0.10$
(upper bound) on its own finding; otherwise \textsc{mixed}. On the false positives, the cardiomegaly
contrast set is compared with random active features, and evidence-block with border-block anchoring,
at the same threshold. Results: specificity \val{e5.S1}; on the false positives \val{e5.S2}, and
\val{e5.S3} for anchoring.
\item \textbf{Live MLPs} (the replication's films, feature sets, seeds and samples). Each
suppression's change to the MLP outputs, the reconstruction from the edited features minus that from
the unedited ones, is added to the live MLP outputs.
\textsc{inconclusive} if under no variant the interval of the drop in $s$ excludes $0$;
\textsc{holds with live MLPs} if $\Pnorm$ misreads with a negative mean under all three variants;
\textsc{frozen-MLP artifact} if it tracks under all three; otherwise \textsc{partial}. Result:
\val{e8.verdict} (\val{e8.scale1.verdict}, \val{e8.ablate.verdict} and \val{e8.scale2.verdict} under
\texttt{scale}~$-1$, \texttt{ablate} and \texttt{scale}~$-2$).
\item \textbf{Live MLPs, weaker suppression.} As above, with each film's features scaled by
$\lambda \in \{0.9, 0.75, 0.6, 0.45, 0.3, 0.15\}$. A strength is eligible if at most \val{e9.tau}
of samples give neither answer or say they cannot see an image (the largest share under any arm of
the replication), and the interval of its drop in $s$ lies above $0.05$. At the eligible strength
whose drop in $s$ is closest to that of clamped \texttt{scale}~$-1$, by the replication's rule:
\textsc{holds live} if $\Pnorm$ misreads with a negative mean, \textsc{reverses live} if with a
positive mean, \textsc{tracks live} if it tracks, otherwise \textsc{unresolved}; \textsc{no matched
dose} if no strength is eligible. Result: \val{e9.verdict}.
\item \textbf{Patient-disjoint donor sets.} Mean pairwise Jaccard at $N{=}80$ above $0.70$:
\textsc{still finding-agnostic}; below $0.50$: \textsc{patient-driven}. Result: \textsc{still
finding-agnostic} (\val{e3.jac.activation.80}). Amplification, on \val{e3.nfilms}
cardiomegaly-negative films with $0.05 < \Praw < 0.5$, each set amplified ten times at image
positions and scored by first-token readouts only: \textsc{no effect} if the cardiomegaly set raises
the probability of cardiomegaly by at most $0.10$ more than random features. Result:
\val{e3.amp.verdict} (\val{e3.amp.vsrandom.norm} above random on $\Pnorm$, \val{e3.amp.vsrandom.raw}
on $\Praw$).
\item \textbf{Pilot-study tests,} scored by first-token readouts only. Readout audit:
\textsc{artifact} (normalized effect under half the raw one), the pilot study's conclusion, which
\S\ref{sec:clamp} revisits.
Occlusion audit: \textsc{survives}. Operator sweep: no operator reaches selectivity $\geq 2\times$.
Evaluation sweep: AUCs under $\Praw$ and $\Pnorm$ within $0.03$. Prompt sweep: no phrasing shifts
$\Praw$ by more than $0.15$ while shifting $\Pnorm$ by less than $0.05$.
\end{itemize}
A separate pre-registered test, of how the readout affects an evaluation with no intervention
(MedGemma and CheXagent-2), asks a different question; its rules and results are in the code
repository.

\section{Radiographs and parsing}
\label{app:sets}
\label{app:parse}

\paragraph{Radiograph sets.} The first false-positive set comes from the pilot study, was selected by
$\Praw \geq 0.5$, and contains two films of one patient. The second was drawn for this paper with
one film per patient. Eligible AP films labelled negative for cardiomegaly were visited in a seeded
random order and accepted when the greedy answer was yes (\val{n.fpfresh} accepted of
\val{scanned.fpfresh} eligible). No patient is in both sets.

\paragraph{Parse rule and hand reading.} The rule strips the characters \texttt{* \_ \# ` >},
lowercases, and returns the first whole word ``yes'' or ``no''. The hand-reading protocol was
committed before the sheet was drawn. The sheet mixed \val{hand.e4.n} samples drawn uniformly from
the replication with the \val{hand.e2.distinct} distinct greedy texts of the first check. The reader,
the author, saw only the question and the reply, and wrote yes, no or neither. The rule agreed on
\val{hand.e4.agree} of \val{hand.e4.n} samples and \val{hand.e2.distinctagree} of
\val{hand.e2.distinct} greedy texts. Of the \val{hand.dis.n} disagreements, \val{hand.dis.rulenone}
answer without the word yes or no (\say{it does not appear to show cardiomegaly}), which the rule
calls neither; one calls the heart enlarged and then says \say{The answer is **No**}; and two say yes
before reaching the length limit, which the sheet's instructions counted as neither. Each is quoted
in the result file.

\paragraph{Sensitivity (not pre-registered).} Counting every answer that gives neither as yes, and
then as no, $\Pnorm$ missing the effect stays \val{sens.yes.V1norms} and \val{sens.no.V1norms},
$\Praw$ stays \val{sens.yes.V1raws} and \val{sens.no.V1raws}, and the fresh-film replication stays
\val{sens.yes.V2} and \val{sens.no.V2}. The true-positive verdict, \val{v.V3s} as parsed, becomes
\val{sens.yes.V3s} when neither counts as yes and stays \val{sens.no.V3s} when it counts as no. Under
three other parse rules (the last yes or no; the first after deleting mentions of the format such
as ``yes or no''; and that rule counting replies that cannot see an image or hit the length limit as
neither), $\Pnorm$ misreads every suppression variant, falling short of $s$ by
\val{alt.norm.min} to \val{alt.norm.max}. Degenerate text is rare except under \texttt{scale}~$-2$,
where \val{text.scale2.trunc} of samples reach the length limit (\val{text.scale1.trunc} under
\texttt{scale}~$-1$, \val{text.base.trunc} unedited). At most \val{text.clamp.noimgmax} of samples in
any variant say they cannot see an image.

\section{The transcoder and reproducibility}
\label{app:instrument}
\label{app:repro}

\paragraph{The transcoder.} Ours, the cross-layer form \citep{lindsey2024crosscoders} of a
transcoder \citep{dunefsky2024transcoders} used for attribution graphs
\citep{ameisen2025circuit,lindsey2025biology}, has \val{clt.used}B trained parameters, $2{,}048$
features per layer across all $34$ layers, and Top-$K$ sparsity with $K{=}32$. The checkpoint also
stores \val{clt.unused}B decoder weights that would write past the last layer and are never used. It
was trained on activations captured while MedGemma processed ChestX-ray14 radiographs (prompt
``Interpret this chest X-ray.'') and, as separate inputs, CheXpert Plus report texts
\citep{chambon2024chexpertplus}. Of the $80$ features edited on each false-positive film, a mean of
\val{clamp.nlast.mean} belong to the last layer; at image positions no later layer reads them, so
editing them cannot change the answer.

\paragraph{Fidelity.} Substituting the reconstruction for every MLP, with no error term, reproduces
the model's top-1 next token on \val{rep.live.agree}\% of \val{rep.n} radiographs under an open-ended
prompt (KL \val{rep.live.kl}). Under our yes/no prompt it does not (\val{rep.yn.agree} of
\val{rep.yn.n}, KL \val{rep.yn.kl}), so every intervention adds back the error term recorded on the
unedited input. On text
positions the dictionary is prompt-bound (fraction of variance unexplained \val{fvu.txt.train} on the
training prompt, \val{fvu.txt.probes} on held-out yes/no prompts), a known failure under distribution
shift \citep{lim2026geometry}. Image-token activations precede the text under causal masking and are
unaffected (\val{fvu.img} on every prompt), and every edit here is at image positions. Checkpoint and
configuration: \url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22105454} (CC BY 4.0; \texttt{clt\_ckpt.pt},
$37{,}630{,}140{,}293$ bytes, MD5 \texttt{4c2747c75e4ce368bd48ccd194f9b8ef}).

\paragraph{Reproducing the suppression.} The first check reran the pilot study's suppression on its
\val{e2.n} films with newer software (PyTorch \val{e2.torch}, Transformers \val{e2.transformers}).
The unedited model's $\Praw$ stayed within \val{e2.base.banked} of the pilot study's; the suppressed
arms differed by up to \val{e2.scale1.banked},
\val{e2.ablate.banked} and \val{e2.scale2.banked}, all on the film where the unedited model drifted
most. On two films the selected feature set changed at the top-$80$ boundary.
% source for the diagnostics below: results/clt_scale/readout_generation_check_amendment.md
On one, the $80$th and $81$st mean activations are $1.0880$ and $1.0812$; on the other, replacing any
one feature of ranks $45$ to $77$ with rank $81$ moves $\Praw$ anywhere from $0.591$ to $0.814$. The
pilot study did not save its feature sets; every later run does. The replication reuses the first
check's sets on those $20$ films and reproduces its first-token readouts to within
\val{repro.e4e2.maxdiff}. As a check on the generation code, the first generated token's
distribution must match the scored forward pass to within $0.02$ in $\Praw$ and $\Pnorm$ on every
radiograph and arm; the largest difference was \val{firsttok.maxdiff}.

\paragraph{Compute.} One H100 (80\,GB). Per their logs in the repository, the generation runs took
\val{compute.e2} min (first check), \val{compute.e4} min (replication) and \val{compute.e5} min
(contrastive selection), some sharing the GPU.
